# Lessons from writing the first one

Each lesson is something Hristo corrected or chose while writing the Core FMS North Star, or
something a reviewer or the squad asked once they saw it, and the rule it leaves behind. The
verbatim versions, with the actual sentences, are in `references/local/` when that folder exists.

## Structure

1. **Put the statement on line one.** The first review of an early draft said there was no
   statement, only themes. Everything else on the page serves one sentence, so it goes first.
2. **Join the statement and the company goal.** They sat in two places and read as two competing
   North Stars. One leads to the other, so say that: the statement, then "If we make this
   statement true, we win. It is how we make ...".
3. **Frame last year's plan, do not just list it.** A lead-in that only introduces bullets
   "feels a bit light". Say when the plan was set, where it stands, how simple it was.
4. **Weave the reason into the move.** A separate list of problems took a third of the page and
   read like a status report. With each problem opening its own move, he said it "reads better".
5. **The foundation goes first.** The enabling move sat last, as work done "while doing the
   above". A reviewer said that if the numbers signal priority it belonged much higher, and when
   he told the story aloud he started with it anyway. It became move one, and the hand-off
   sentence now says the others depend on it.
6. **Let the foundation's "So, in short" line be the thing.** The foundation gets a value
   headline in the body, and the plain description of the work in the closing list.

## Headlines and statements

7. **Explicit beats clever.** A headline with rhythm was "a bit flowery", not explicit about what
   it means for the user. Say what the user can do or stops having to do.
8. **No aphorisms.** A neat boundary sentence "doesn't sound like me". Write it the way he would
   say it to a colleague, or leave it out.
9. **Check what a headline implies.** One suggested that users keep the vendors the product is
   meant to replace. Another suggested users must connect the company's own products. Both were
   true to the letter and wrong in what they implied.
10. **Do not headline the examples.** Named integrations and vendors are derivatives of the
    thing. Headline the thing they are examples of.
11. **One theme per headline.** He did not want a second headline about the theme of an earlier
    move, even though the two moves are linked.
12. **When stuck, ask for the thing in two bullets.** After several rejected headlines he wrote
    the essence himself in two plain bullets. The headline came from those words. Ask for that
    earlier.
13. **The punch line is the outcome of doing it.** A correct headline can still "miss the big
    bang". Offer the outcome three ways (the user's business, the user's team, the company) and
    place the chosen one at the top of the last beat.
14. **Give his long candidate two forms.** When his own sentence has the right content and the
    wrong shape, keep the content and offer it once as a statement and once as a short headline.
    He will hear which one the reviewer is after.

## Sentences and words

15. **He edits one word at a time.** "That word is wrong here" is normal feedback. Offer the
    word his own page already uses for that idea before inventing a new one.
16. **Do not over-extend his rules.** A ban on a filler word was applied to an established term
    that contains it, and he corrected that. Record a rule in his words, mark your own
    interpretation as yours, and ask when a case is unclear.
17. **His wording wins the second round.** If he pastes his version back after a cleaning, fix
    only grammar and name the fix.
18. **"We" at the top, "I" where it counts.** A first-person opening was replaced by "we",
    because it "brings the team together". The first person stays where he owns a contested call.

## Numbers and evidence

19. **A number carries its assumption.** A revenue estimate held only for one payment method.
    The fix was three words in the same sentence. Say such a thing once and then follow his call.
20. **Check how the product works in the code.** He doubted one clause, asked for a check, and
    the code showed the claim was true for one product in four. The corrected sentence became
    the sharpest fact in that move.
21. **Read the neighbours' pages.** Two other squads' drafts carried the same idea. That meant
    the idea was right, that the reviewer would ask which part is his, and that their stated
    needs were ready-made evidence of dependency.
22. **Keep the reviewers' own words.** Their earlier comments predict the next ones better than
    any rubric. Keep them in a private file and quote them when flagging a risk.
23. **Do not work around a blocked source.** When a public page refuses automated reads, say so,
    work from what you know and label it. Ask him to paste the text if precision matters.

## Process

24. **A go is specific.** Page edits need a yes to that edit. After an edit, list what changed,
    what did not, and what of yours is unconfirmed.
25. **He edits the page while you work.** Icons, heading levels, a sentence. Fetch before every
    edit and keep what he did.
26. **Compressed bullets cost a round.** Twice he answered "what? explain". Status lines need a
    full sentence each.
27. **Examples, not frameworks.** Asked for ways to structure a story, a list of methods got "I
    don't want a course or a book". He wanted famous concrete examples he could copy.
28. **Write the notes as you go.** The session ran long enough to lose its context more than
    once. His raw notes, each decision and the state of the page were in memory, so nothing was
    lost.

## Visuals

29. **Rows beat the diagram.** Offered a hub-and-spoke version in the style of a famous strategy
    chart, he preferred the simpler slide with rows.
30. **Let it breathe without cutting.** "I want the slide to breathe" was first answered by
    cutting text. He wanted the text back and the room found in the layout: title on one row,
    the supporting line on the second, more space between blocks.
31. **Brand guide over a liked deck.** Where the official brand rules and a deck he liked
    differed in font and colour, the brand rules won, with the deck's layout cues kept. Tell him
    which is which.

## After the reviewers read it

32. **Order is read as priority.** He said the numbers were not a ranking. A reviewer ranked by
    them anyway. Put the moves in the order he would defend.
33. **A move must differ from last year's recap in its first sentence.** The recap said users
    could already do what one move promised, in the same words. The head of product asked how
    that was different from now. The phrase left the recap, and the move now opens with what
    people still do by hand.
34. **Fix and ambition in the same move.** A move about making something reliable was read as
    mostly defensive. A paragraph on what the reliable thing lets the product become answered it.
35. **Every advantage gets "why can the incumbent not do this?"** Answer with something
    structural in his words, or do not claim the advantage. For one move he chose to say only why
    the work is needed: to stay competitive and to serve larger customers well.
36. **An abstract sentence gets "what does this mean?"** The sentence that drew it was replaced
    by the mechanism: what is written back, what the product then completes, and which
    exceptions become a task for whom.
37. **"What if we just don't do it next year?" decides what is a move.** A theme with a good
    story and little cost of delay went to an appendix, "for future consideration". One sentence
    of it stayed in the move it supports.
38. **Small and bounded is a feature, not direction.** His own reply showed that a theme needed
    only a modest change, and the reviewer asked whether it was North Star at all. He agreed: it
    keeps the North Star "directional, not tactical". It went to an appendix as "a functionality
    we add".
39. **Have the size ready.** For a theme a reviewer suspects is a rabbit hole, know the last
    estimate: how many tickets, how many weeks, how many people.
40. **Name the joint bet.** Two people separately called one theme another squad's play. One
    sentence on who decides what showed which part is his.
41. **A reviewer's "keep it out" stands.** A topic one reviewer raised in the session was taken
    off the page by the same reviewer a day later. Stop proposing it.
42. **His decisions close a flag.** On who owns a surface, and on whether a move needs a
    competitor answer, he decided. Record the decision and do not raise the flag again.
43. **Examples, not levels.** A paragraph that sorted a scope into three levels was rejected. He
    replaced it with one concrete example of what "basic" means.

## Evidence, second round

44. **His own sentence can be false.** A sentence he wrote about how the product behaves today
    was carried into a draft unchecked. The code said the opposite on the main point. Check
    before it goes in. A ticket title is not proof.
45. **Scope the absolute.** "Never saved" was true on the page he tested and false for the
    product as a whole. It became "not saved", plus the one place where it was verified, and a
    dated check on staging went into the evidence.
46. **Find what his number covers before calling it off.** Twice a spoken number looked wrong
    and was right on his baseline: an earlier start date, and delivered work counted with open
    work.
47. **Write down what the sources do not show.** A competitor incident grew a little with each
    telling. The evidence doc now gives the wording the source supports and lists the claims it
    does not.
48. **One bullet per source, ending with the move it backs.** The evidence went into a collapsed
    appendix. The body keeps one sentence of proof per beat.
49. **A private link is not evidence.** Docs and dashboards that only he can open have to be
    shared before a reviewer can use them.

## Telling it

50. **Assume nobody read the page.** In the first review session no one had. The slide carried
    the plan.
51. **Tell it in the page's words.** In the first telling he opened with the opposite of the
    page's position on the competitor, and told one move without what is broken today. Both
    drew questions the page had already answered. In the second telling both were fixed.
52. **Two moves told in the same words are heard as one.** Aloud, the foundation was explained
    with the promise of the move it enables. Give each move one sentence only it can claim.
53. **Fifteen to eighteen minutes, then the room.** The second telling stopped at eighteen
    minutes and asked for impressions. Better still, stop after the statement and after each
    move.
54. **Explain the method to people new to it.** For a squad that had never worked with a North
    Star, a minute on what it is and how it is used to say no was time well spent.
55. **A new name sounds like new work.** An engineer heard the company initiative behind one move
    as a second redesign in two years. One sentence on what it continues and who gets it first
    went onto the page.
56. **What he says in the room belongs on the page or nowhere.** List the plans and dates he
    mentioned that the page does not carry, and let him choose.
57. **Score the presentation and the discussion separately.** Counting his answers in the
    discussion as airtime produced a wrong score, and he corrected it. A claim built on a garbled
    word in the transcript was wrong as well.

## Process, second round

58. **A review copy before the page.** For the restructure he reviewed a local file in which
    every sentence was marked as his page text, his words from replies, or mine. Only then did
    the page change.
59. **Comment threads survive a restructure if you carry them.** Six reviewer threads stayed
    attached through a full rewrite, each on its matching text, and the reviewer's reactions too.
60. **A reorder breaks sentences elsewhere.** The hand-off, a first-person line that justified
    the old order, the closing list and the evidence endings all needed fixing after one change.
61. **A go without a named change is not a go for a deletion.** Say which single change is
    pending and wait for the word.
62. **The slide moves with the page.** When the foundation became move one, the solid base row
    went to the top of the slide, and the previous image and its source were kept under a dated
    name.
