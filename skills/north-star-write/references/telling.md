# Telling it, and reading the session afterwards

Leadership asks each PM to tell the North Star, with the doc as the fallback. Then he tells it
again to his own squad. The page and the telling have to say the same thing. When they differ,
the room asks exactly the questions the page had already answered.

## Before the session

- **Assume nobody has read the page.** In the first review session no one had. The slide carried
  the plan, and the head of product checked that the whole conclusion was on it.
- **About fifteen minutes, with stops.** Stop after the statement, around five minutes in, and
  ask for the first reaction. Then stop after each move. Eighteen minutes of presenting is the
  ceiling. TED caps a talk there and the 10/20/30 rule says twenty.
- **One sentence per move that only that move can claim.** Write them with him beforehand. If
  the foundation and the move it enables are explained with the same promise, the room hears one
  move.
- **The page's order.** If he would start somewhere else when speaking, the page's order is
  wrong. Fix the page, not the telling.
- **Competitors and numbers exactly as written.** Give him the safe sentence for each: the one
  the evidence supports, with the count attributed to whoever counted it. A spoken version that
  grows a little each time is the easiest thing for a reviewer to catch.
- **What is broken today comes before next year.** A move told only as the future draws "how is
  that different from now?".

## Two rooms

- **Leadership review.** They are testing it. Expect the questions in
  `review-rounds.md`. A clear yes on one move and silence on another are both answers. Write
  both down.
- **His own squad.** They want it to work and they think about the how. For people who have not
  worked with a North Star, a minute on the method is worth it. His strongest version: it is what
  we use to say no, shown with one request that fits and one that does not. Close on what
  happens next and who works out the how with whom. Expect "is this another redesign?" for
  anything with a new name, and have the answer ready: what it builds on and who gets it first.

## In the room

- Give the one-line answer first. Then ask if they want the detail.
- "What do you think?" brings the same three voices. Ask one person one specific question.
- When a challenge has a valid half, say which half. Then answer the rest.

## Afterwards: read the transcript against the page

Get the recording's transcript and the page as it stood during the session. Report six things:

1. **The outcome.** A verdict, buy-in, or neither. Whether anyone had read the page.
2. **Where the telling matched the page.** Name the parts, so he keeps them.
3. **Where it drifted.** The opening, the order, a move told without its reason, two moves told
   as one.
4. **Facts that differ from the evidence.** Quote what he said and what the evidence says.
5. **What he said that the page does not carry.** Plans, dates and commitments made in the room.
   They belong on the page or nowhere. Let him choose.
6. **What was asked, by whom, and who said nothing.** The questions are the next draft's tests.

Then offer the fixes as sentences, built from his own spoken words. Say that they are his, cleaned
or translated. Nothing goes on the page without his go.

## Scoring a session fairly

He will ask how it went, and sometimes for a number. A wrong score costs more trust than no score.

- **Time the presentation and the discussion separately.** Presenting ends when he asks the room
  for questions. His answers in the discussion are not airtime.
- **Compare like with like.** Presenting minutes against presenting minutes. A friendly room
  against a testing room is not the same exam, so say so.
- **Before listing a spoken number as wrong, find what it covers.** Which date, which
  population, open work or delivered work too. Check it on his baseline first.
- **Do not build a claim on a garbled word.** Transcripts mangle product and company names, more
  so when the meeting is not in English. Quote only what is clear and mark a guess as a guess.
- **Explaining the method to people new to it is not a flaw.** Judge the framing by the audience.
- **Speaker labels can be wrong.** One room microphone may be tagged with one person's name for
  several voices. Match voices by how he addresses them, and say that you did.
- If he rates an earlier session himself, use his number as the anchor and say what moved the
  new one up and what held it back.
