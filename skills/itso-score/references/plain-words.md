# Plain words, to the point, and the so what

Where these rules come from: the NFX talk "Magic Words" (Speed Masterclass, episode 5), which
his head of product assigned. Its argument is language first, product second: say what the
thing is to the user, in words the user would say. Hristo wrote his North Star by that test,
rejected line after line until each one passed, and now wants every text held to it. The same
rules sit in the companion skill `north-star-write`.

Three parts: the words, the structures, the so what.

## 1. The words: explicit beats clever

**The test.** Would he say this sentence out loud to a colleague, and would the reader repeat
it in their own words? If it only works written down, it is copy, not him.

- **Say what the reader or user gets, literally.** A verb, a concrete object, a concrete
  benefit. "Pay an invoice in two clicks" passes. "Payments, reimagined" does not.
- **No slogans and no aphorisms.** Three-beat rhythm, "X is the line", "without surprises",
  "from A to B" pairs and similar read as copywriting. If a line sounds quotable, rewrite it
  as a plain statement. His words for the miss: "a bit flowery", "doesn't sound like me".
- **Separate the thing from the value.** "A model that supports ..." is the thing. What the
  user can now do is the value. Lead with the value. The thing can follow in the same
  sentence or the next.
- **Read it for what it implies.** A line can be true and still suggest the wrong thing: that
  the reader has to do work they should not, or that the product does less than it does. Ask
  what a skeptical reader would infer.
- **Name the thing, not a stand-in.** A vague noun used for several different things makes
  every sentence read as the same claim. He banned one such catch-all by name. Say which
  thing: the data, the system, the page, the API. An established term that happens to
  contain the word is fine. Do not stretch a ban past what he said.
- **One example beats a framework.** Asked how far something goes, he answers with the
  example. "Levels", "tiers", "pillars" and "dimensions" are a framework reflex.
- **Scope the absolutes.** "Never", "no", "all" and "every" claim the whole product or the
  whole customer base. Keep the claim the size of what is known.
- **Pick the word that claims the right amount.** He edits one word at a time. A verb that
  overclaims, or an adverb that sounds technical, gets replaced by the plainer one. When
  unsure, use the word his own text already uses for that idea.
- **Inside words cost outside readers.** Feature names, squad shorthand and ticket language
  are fine for the squad and wrong for a customer or an exec.

## 2. The structures: to the point

- **The outcome goes first.** In a reply: the outcome, then the block he can paste, then a
  short list of touches. In a doc: the statement on line one, then the reason.
- **Each part leads with its conclusion.** A section, a paragraph and a bullet all open with
  the claim. Evidence follows. A reader who stops after the first line still has the point.
- **Bold leads assert.** A bold lead-in is a full claim with a verb, not a one-word label.
- **Today, next, why it matters.** His moves run in three beats: what is true today, what it
  looks like when it works, why it holds. The same order serves an update or a proposal.
- **A closing list repeats, it does not add.** His "So, in short" is the headlines again.
  Nothing new arrives at the end.
- **Short sentences, one idea each.** No parentheticals as a habit, no chains of dashes, no
  arrows. A list only for a list. A table only to compare.
- **Terse, not cryptic.** Cut words, never the verb. When a bullet needs "what? explain", it
  was compressed past a sentence. Each line stays a plain, whole sentence.
- **"We" at the top, "I" where he owns a call.** "We" brings the team in. "I" marks a
  judgment someone could disagree with. Two or three in a long text, not more.
- **Length follows stakes.** One line for a Slack reply. A long, headed email when the matter
  is an escalation with a dozen facts in it.

## 3. The so what

A text can be short, plain and still pointless. The reader's next question tells you: "so
what?", "what does this mean?", "how is that different from today?", "what do you need from
me?". Answer it in the text.

- **The punch line is the outcome of doing it.** When a line is correct and still flat, he
  asks "what's the punch line?". He means what changes: for the reader's business, for their
  team, or for the company. Pick the one that matters to this reader and say it.
- **A number carries its conclusion.** A figure is not evidence until the sentence says what
  to conclude from it. A number that does not prove the sentence it sits in goes. If it rests
  on an assumption, the assumption is in the same sentence.
- **A finding is followed by "so".** "X is the case, so we do Y." Without the second half it
  is a status, and the reader has to work out the decision.
- **A difference is stated against today.** If the text describes a future, the first
  sentence says what is still true now. Otherwise the reader asks how it differs.
- **End on the ask or the next step, with its owner.** Not on a summary, a moral, or an offer
  to help.
- **One clause is usually enough.** "So unless you see a reason to hold, we don't block the
  release on it." The so what is not a paragraph about significance.
- **Do not invent it.** If the draft has no consequence in it and you cannot derive one from
  its facts, say what is missing and ask him.

## Quick check before a text goes out

1. Does the first line carry the point?
2. Could every sentence be said out loud to a colleague?
3. Is there a line that sounds quotable? Rewrite it plainly.
4. Does each number say what to conclude?
5. After the last line, does the reader know what happens next and who does it?
6. Is it shorter than the last version, and is each line still a whole sentence?
